Treating lunar water: which Earth methods would work

Water treatment on Earth relies on gravity, air and chemicals delivered by truck. On the Moon gravity is six times weaker, there is no air, and every kilogram of reagent flies from Earth. A process engineer looks at which familiar methods get easier, which get harder, and what is new.

November 26, 2026 · Moon, Water treatment, Space

Water treatment on Earth leans on gravity and air, which are free, and on chemicals a truck brings every week. On the Moon gravity is about six times weaker, there’s practically no atmosphere, and every kilogram of coagulant or resin has to be launched from Earth.

In the previous article I wrote that lunar ice may carry hydrogen sulphide, ammonia, methanol and fine dust, if it’s as dirty as the one impact measurement from 2009 suggests. Here’s how familiar methods would fare.

What gets easier

Outside the vessels, vacuum and deep cold come free, so a cold trap at the right temperature can freeze water out of the vapour while hydrogen sulphide and other more volatile gases stay in the gas. The US company Paragon has lab-tested such a trap as a first cleanup stage. Distillation travels well too: since 2008 the International Space Station has distilled urine in a spinning drum, where rotation stands in for gravity.

What gets harder

The same particle in the same water would settle about six times slower (my estimate from Stokes’ law), so filters and centrifuges replace settling tanks. Coagulants, resins, activated carbon and chlorine must be regenerated on site or flown in.

The catches

Liquid water can’t exist in open vacuum, so every step runs in closed vessels, some below atmospheric pressure. Ammonia and methanol mix with water, and I’d expect them to follow it into the condensate. In a German lab test, about 1% methanol, added as a worst case, formed a liquid film on the cold trap and cut water capture.

The station shows how chemistry can surprise you. In 2009 gypsum built up in its urine distiller, as calcium from the crew’s bone loss met sulphate from the sulphuric acid in pretreatment. NASA cut water recovery from urine from 85% to 70% and returned to 85% only in 2016, after switching to phosphoric acid. The fix was a change of reagent.

The chain I’d expect

As a process engineer, I see a chain much like the station’s: a cold trap up front, then distillation, catalytic oxidation for the organics that slip through, and ion exchange polishing. What’s new is how it runs. A plant in a dark crater far from the base can’t wait for an operator, yet even on the station only some checks run inline and samples still fly to Earth. On the Moon, analysers and automation would have to make most of the calls.
