Operator error that starts on the drawings: chemical unloading at water plants

An unloading mix-up in Allentown sent five people to hospital. Incidents like it look like operator errors, but many of them were set up years earlier, in the design of the chemical area.

November 16, 2026 · Process safety, Chemical dosing, Hypochlorite

What happened in Allentown

Earlier this month a delivery of sodium hypochlorite at a water treatment plant in Allentown, Pennsylvania, went into the wrong tank. That tank held polyaluminum chloride. Five people underwent precautionary decontamination and were taken to hospital, the vapour stayed inside the building, and a half-mile shelter-in-place was lifted the same evening. Nobody reported symptoms.

The chemistry is simple. PAC is acidic, and hypochlorite mixed with acid releases chlorine gas. Every water plant that uses both chemicals has this risk on site, usually a few metres apart.

Operator error, or design error?

The Chlorine Institute’s Pamphlet 167 “Learning From Experience” collects incidents from chlorine producers and users. Read as headlines, many of them are operator errors. Read in detail, a lot of them were made possible by the design.

Fill lines side by side. In one case hydrochloric acid was unloaded into a hypochlorite tank because several product lines sat next to each other. The operator died a couple of weeks later. In another, during a shift change, a driver connected sulfuric acid to a hypochlorite tank. The fill lines were within 0.6 m of each other. The driver couldn’t stop the flow, and the valve was closed only by first responders in breathing apparatus. Chlorine also reached the control room through the air conditioning intake (sections 3.1 and 3.3).

Tanks and bunds without labels. At a water treatment plant, hypochlorite went into an unlabelled tank of fluorosilicic acid, although the driver questioned it more than once (section 3.2). At a new facility, operators drained the wrong bund into a swale: it held 1.9 to 3.8 m³ of 10% caustic, and nothing on site told the two bunds apart (section 6.3).

No high-level alarm. About 41.6 m³ of 50% caustic overflowed a storage tank when a transfer ran into the next shift. The corrective action was a high-level alarm, and the lesson was redundant level indication (section 14.2).

Materials that don’t match the duty. A fitting on a caustic sampling port could not take the process temperature. The pamphlet says directly that the original design did not evaluate that equipment properly (section 8.2).

A bund that didn’t hold. About 159 m³ of 36% hydrochloric acid escaped when a storage tank failed. The tank sat inside a dike, but the acid channelled underneath it (section 14.3).

In each case someone on site made the last mistake. But the layout, the missing alarm or the wrong material had been on the drawings long before that day.

What I put on the drawings

When I design chemical systems for water plants, I treat these as design questions, not operating ones.

  • Incompatible chemicals get separate fill points and separate bunds, physically apart, so the wrong connection is hard to make by accident.
  • Every tank and every fill coupling is labelled at the point where the hose goes on, not only on the tank shell.
  • Fill valves are locked, and the plant decides who holds the keys.
  • High-level alarms don’t depend on the level control loop, so a single failed transmitter can’t hide an overflow.
  • Materials are checked against the real concentration and temperature of each chemical, including fittings and sampling points.
  • Air intakes for control rooms and buildings stay away from the unloading area.

None of this removes human error. Operators and drivers will still make mistakes. The design decides whether a mistake stays a near miss or becomes an incident.

Questions to ask about your own plant

If you run a plant with hypochlorite and an acidic coagulant or acid on site, walk the unloading area with these questions:

  • Can a driver who has never been here tell the fill points apart without asking anyone?
  • How far apart are the couplings for incompatible chemicals?
  • Who holds the keys to the fill valves, and who checks the line before the hose is connected?
  • What tells the operator a tank is about to overflow if the level transmitter fails?
  • Where does the air for the control room come from?

If any answer is “it depends who’s on shift”, that is a design question worth reopening.
