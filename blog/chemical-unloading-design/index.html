Operator error that starts on the drawings: chemical unloading at water plants

An unloading mix-up in Allentown sent five people to hospital. Incidents like it look like operator errors, but many of them were set up years earlier, in the design of the chemical area.

November 16, 2026 · Process safety, Chemical dosing, Hypochlorite

What happened in Allentown

Earlier this month a delivery of sodium hypochlorite at a water treatment plant in Allentown, Pennsylvania, went into the wrong tank. That tank held polyaluminum chloride. Five people underwent precautionary decontamination and were taken to hospital, the vapour stayed inside the building, and a half-mile shelter-in-place was lifted the same evening. Nobody reported symptoms.

The chemistry is simple. PAC is acidic, and hypochlorite mixed with acid releases chlorine gas. Every water plant that uses both chemicals has this risk on site, usually a few metres apart.

Operator error, or design error?

The Chlorine Institute’s Pamphlet 167 “Learning From Experience” collects incidents from chlorine producers and users. Read as headlines, many of them are operator errors. Read in detail, a lot of them were made possible by the design. Below are seven cases retold in my own words, with the section and page of the pamphlet.

P167 · 3.1 · circa 2008 · p. 8

Hydrochloric acid into a bleach tank

What happened
At a facility that uses bleach, hydrochloric acid was unloaded into a sodium hypochlorite tank. Chlorine evolved, the operator collapsed and died a couple of weeks later. Two colleagues who rescued him were also hospitalised, and fifteen more people went to hospital for observation.
Design cause
Several product lines were located side by side, which contributed to the confusion.
What changed
The investigation report was not available to the Chlorine Institute. The pamphlet points to its self-assessment checklist for hypochlorite users.

P167 · 3.2 · 2013 · p. 9

Hypochlorite into an unlabelled acid tank

What happened
At a water treatment plant, the operator directed a driver to unload hypochlorite into a tank that held fluorosilicic acid. The driver questioned it more than once and was assured it was the right tank. Vapour came off the tank; the driver and several operators were hospitalised.
Design cause
The storage tank was unmarked and unlabelled.
What changed
The distributor's drivers no longer unload into an unlabelled tank until a supervisor or manager at the site confirms it. The case went into driver training.

P167 · 3.3 · October 2016 · p. 9–10

Sulfuric acid into a hypochlorite tank

What happened
During a shift change, a driver connected sulfuric acid to the hypochlorite tank. Chlorine filled the area. The driver could not stop the flow, the gas was drawn into the control room through the air conditioning intake, and the operators' escape respirators were locked away. First responders in breathing apparatus closed the valve.
Design cause
Fill lines to different tanks were within 0.6 m of each other. The gas reached the control room through its air conditioning intake.
What changed
The loading area was redesigned, incompatible chemicals were physically separated, and the keys to unlock valves were controlled more tightly. The chemical supplier also set stricter requirements for its customers' receiving process.

P167 · 6.3 · April 2021 · p. 18

The wrong bund drained

What happened
At a new facility, operators were told to drain rainwater from a tank farm bund. They drained the wrong one: it held about 1.9 to 3.8 m³ of 10.2% caustic, which went into a swale. Nobody noticed until the grass died.
Design cause
There were no unique identifiers, so nothing on site told one bund from another.
What changed
Unique signs and labels for drainage, revised procedures and training, and a hazard communication audit.

P167 · 14.2 · May 1991 · p. 43

Caustic tank overflow

What happened
About 41.6 m³ of 50% caustic overflowed a storage tank into its bund. A storm water valve was partly open, and about 0.4 m³ escaped to the environment. The transfer had not been logged and ran on into the next shift.
Design cause
There was no high-level alarm to catch a transfer nobody was watching: one was installed only afterwards.
What changed
A high-level alarm was installed. The lesson: caustic storage tanks should have redundant level indication and alarms.

P167 · 8.2 · December 2007 · p. 25

A sampling port that leaked

What happened
A caustic leak of about 36 kg was spotted at a sampling port on an electrolyser when nobody was taking a sample. It was handled without injuries.
Design cause
The fitting was not compatible with high-temperature caustic, and the sample valve was defective. The pamphlet says the original process design did not evaluate the equipment specified for the port.
What changed
The equipment was upgraded. Equipment must suit the material at the actual temperature and pressure.

P167 · 14.3 · October 2004 · p. 43–44

Acid under the dike

What happened
About 159 m³ of 36% hydrochloric acid escaped when a storage tank failed at a recently repaired rubber-lined patch. The release lasted about 70 minutes and reached the ground and a public wastewater treatment works.
Design cause
The tank stood inside a dike, but the acid channelled underneath it and left the containment.
What changed
Repair procedures for rubber lining were revised and the dike was upgraded. The lesson: check that containment will actually hold a spill, and inspect it regularly.

In each case someone on site made the last mistake. But the layout, the missing label or alarm, or the wrong material had been on the drawings long before that day. The full descriptions are in the pamphlet itself, available from the Chlorine Institute.

What I put on the drawings

When I design chemical systems for water plants, I treat these as design questions, not operating ones.

  • Incompatible chemicals get separate fill points and separate bunds, physically apart, so the wrong connection is hard to make by accident.
  • Every tank and every fill coupling is labelled at the point where the hose goes on, not only on the tank shell.
  • Fill valves are locked, and the plant decides who holds the keys.
  • High-level alarms don’t depend on the level control loop, so a single failed transmitter can’t hide an overflow.
  • Materials are checked against the real concentration and temperature of each chemical, including fittings and sampling points.
  • Air intakes for control rooms and buildings stay away from the unloading area.

None of this removes human error. Operators and drivers will still make mistakes. The design decides whether a mistake stays a near miss or becomes an incident.

Questions to ask about your own plant

If you run a plant with hypochlorite and an acidic coagulant or acid on site, walk the unloading area with these questions:

  • Can a driver who has never been here tell the fill points apart without asking anyone?
  • How far apart are the couplings for incompatible chemicals?
  • Who holds the keys to the fill valves, and who checks the line before the hose is connected?
  • What tells the operator a tank is about to overflow if the level transmitter fails?
  • Where does the air for the control room come from?

If any answer is “it depends who’s on shift”, that is a design question worth reopening.
