Water from lunar ice: what it takes and how clean it is

One tonne of water from lunar ice means digging at least 20 tonnes of soil, then heating it in permanent darkness. I look at the numbers from NASA and the German Aerospace Center as a water engineer, and at what is likely to be in the water.

November 25, 2026 · Moon, Water treatment, Space

How much soil for a tonne of water

To get one tonne of water from lunar ice, you’d have to dig up at least 20 tonnes of soil. That’s NASA’s own target for lunar excavation: 1,000 tonnes of icy soil for 50 tonnes of water, assuming about 5% ice and that all of it is recovered. A 2020 NASA design study assumed 75% recovery, which brings it closer to 27 tonnes (my calculation).

What got me thinking was the news. On 25 November the Japanese space agency JAXA showed a test model of its new lunar rover. It’s part of a joint mission with India, planned for 2028 at the earliest, to find out how much water lies near the Moon’s south pole and in what form.

The energy bill

Digging is only the start. The ice sits in craters that never see sunlight, so all the heat has to be brought in. At the German Aerospace Center (DLR), researchers heated a simulant of icy lunar soil in a vacuum chamber and caught the vapour on a cold surface. The whole rig used 15 to 44 kWh for every kilogram of water it recovered, depending on the run. That’s enough to boil 160 to 470 litres of water in a kettle (my calculation), spent on one litre of lunar water, and that’s before anyone digs anything.

What will be in the water

The water won’t be clean either. In 2009 NASA crashed a spent rocket stage into a crater near the south pole and measured the cloud it threw up. Next to the water vapour there was a lot of hydrogen sulphide, roughly 17 molecules for every 100 of water, and some ammonia. It’s an early estimate from a single impact, but it gives an idea.

The German team saw what impurities can do. They added about 1% methanol to the ice as a worst case, and it condensed as a liquid film on the cold surface, where less water was then captured. They also changed the stirring in that run, so methanol may not be the only cause. NASA’s 2020 study left water cleanup out entirely, because the contaminants weren’t known yet.

My view

As a water engineer, I’d say this water is closer to a sour process condensate than to anything from a river. I’d try to separate the hydrogen sulphide and ammonia while the vapour is being captured, before the ice is ever melted.
