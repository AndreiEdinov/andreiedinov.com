AI or a PLC? What a GEA decanter already has, and what vendors call predictive maintenance

TPO writes about machine learning moving treatment plants to planned repairs. I compared that with what an ordinary GEA decanter already has according to its documentation, and with what the vendors themselves sell as predictive maintenance. Plus a decanter repair where a PLC and opening the machine did the job, with no AI.

November 19, 2026 · Predictive maintenance, Sludge dewatering, Decanter

Why I’m wary of AI for everything

A lot is being written about AI at treatment plants. This week TPO (Treatment Plant Operator, a US trade magazine for water and wastewater operators) ran an article on how machine learning is moving plants from emergency repairs to planned ones.

I’m not against the idea itself. But before buying AI, it helps to know what is already fitted on the machine. I took a decanter I had repaired and opened the documentation GEA supplies with the machine.

What a GEA decanter already has

The machine: a GEA WATERMASTER CF 4000 for sludge dewatering. Everything below comes from its documentation.

GEA also gives a scale for the vibration level (RMS, to ISO 2372, p. 815):

Vibration, mm/sGEA assessment
0–7good
7–18acceptable
18–20still acceptable, but for no more than 250 hours of operation
above 20not permissible

So an ordinary machine already has measurement, limits in the PLC, a trend on the panel and an event log. What it doesn’t have is the answer to “why”. The sensor gives one overall number along one axis. It doesn’t give a spectrum, so it can say that the machine shakes, but not what causes it.

Why the number can’t name the cause

The GEA troubleshooting table lists four causes for vibration that is too high (p. 823):

  • one-sided product deposits
  • damaged rolling bearings
  • loose fits
  • deposits rubbing against rotating parts

The description of the machine names the two most common ones directly: product deposits and rolling bearing wear (p. 743). The control unit manual adds one more action to the vibration fault: check the feed volume and consistency (p. 167–168). All of these push the same RMS number up. The screen can’t tell you which one it is.

A repair where a PLC and opening the machine were enough

In 2023 I repaired a decanter like this at the wastewater plant of a food producer. Vibration had reached 18 to 20 mm/s, which is GEA’s warning and shutdown range. The climb was visible on the control panel trend. Nobody needed a model to notice it. Nobody could name the cause from the screen.

We opened it up:

  • lifted out the bowl and scroll and stripped them to the assembly drawing
  • cleaned the deposits off the scroll
  • replaced bearings, seals, drive shaft, belts and the speed sensor, 27 items from the parts list

After start-up the machine’s own vibration sensors read 6 to 7 mm/s, within GEA’s “good” band of up to 7. Photos of the strip-down are in the repair case.

We only saw the deposits on the scroll once the machine was open. A single overall number from the sensor can’t tell them apart from bearing wear: both causes push the same RMS value up.

What vendors call predictive maintenance

GEA itself, in the documentation for this machine, offers its wewatch® condition monitoring system for predictive maintenance: continuous data collection on the machine’s key parameters and a condition-based maintenance schedule (p. 64). Today GEA’s website lists InsightPartner Separation Monitoring for separators and decanters. Two to three additional sensors are fitted to the machine. They measure vibration velocity and acceleration at different points and, as GEA puts it, only measure and do not switch the centrifuge off. Data goes to the GEA cloud or to the machine’s panel: trends, anomaly detection, diagnostics and remote support from GEA experts.

ANDRITZ describes its VibePro sensor as combining vibration with process data, with spectrum (FFT) analysis and fault libraries. Spectrum analysis on its own is a classic vibration diagnostics method: it splits vibration by frequency and needs no machine learning. Alfa Laval sells ConditionAlert for decanters, where vibration data is analysed remotely and anomalies are reported under a service agreement.

None of these pages says there is machine learning inside. All three build on the same things: more sensors, more than one number, data history and people who read it.

I haven’t used these systems on a decanter, so I can’t say how much they add over the standard protection. If one is offered for your plant, I’d ask the vendor plainly: which signals does it use beyond overall vibration, can it tell deposits from bearing damage before the machine is opened, and on how many comparable machines has that been shown.

Before buying AI, check your machine’s documentation

  • Which sensors are fitted and what exactly do they measure: velocity or acceleration, how many axes?
  • Which limits are set in the control unit, and what happens at each stage?
  • Is a vibration trend set up on the panel, and how long is it kept?
  • When was the vibration protection last function-tested? GEA’s maintenance schedule says every 4,000 hours, and at least every 12 months (p. 698).
  • How many operating hours are on the bearings, seals and belts against the manufacturer’s schedule? GEA has the belts and the bowl and scroll bearings, together with all their seals, replaced every 8,000 hours, and at least every three years (p. 699).

If you can’t answer these, a model won’t answer them for you.
