PFAS in Georgia, part 2: a hypothetical brief for 2030

If Georgia adopted the EU limit for PFAS in drinking water, what would a large utility have to do? A hypothetical brief for 2030: sampling, treatment, disposal, costs and seven real cases.

November 24, 2026 · PFAS, Drinking water, Water treatment

Since 12 January 2026, EU drinking water has had PFAS limits: 0.10 µg/L for the sum of 20 listed PFAS, and 0.50 µg/L for PFAS Total once EU monitoring guidelines for that parameter exist. Member States may use either or both. In the Georgian texts I checked (Resolutions No. 58 for drinking water, No. 334 and No. 425 for discharges) there is no PFAS parameter.

In part 1 I looked at the rules. Here I try an exercise: if Georgia adopted the EU limit, what would a large utility have to do? The utility is hypothetical and its PFAS numbers are invented. No PFAS measurements from Georgian water are public, so nothing here points to a problem. The technology, the calculations and the reference projects are real.

The brief

It’s 2030. Georgia has adopted EU-style PFAS limits for drinking water. A large utility needs a compliance concept. Part of its water is surface water with a conventional treatment train: coagulation, filtration and chlorine. Part is groundwater with disinfection only. It also runs a wastewater plant.

Why a conventional train won’t do it

Neither process was designed to remove PFAS. The US EPA states that coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, disinfection and filtration do not remove PFAS found in source water.

Wastewater adds a twist. Biological treatment is designed for organic matter (BOD), nitrogen and phosphorus, and it removes perfluoroalkyl acids (PFAAs) poorly. Some other PFAS, called precursors, transform into PFAAs along the way, so PFAA levels at the outlet can be higher than at the inlet. Targeted analysis sees the PFAAs but not most precursors, so inlet and outlet samples alone can under- or overestimate removal. Treated wastewater is one of the sources of PFAS in surface water.

What works

At full scale, PFAS are mostly removed by separation:

  • Granular activated carbon (GAC). Short chains break through first.
  • Anion exchange resin. Selective resins hold short-chain sulfonates such as PFBS better than carbon; when both are used, resin usually follows carbon.
  • Reverse osmosis and nanofiltration. They remove PFAS well, and the EPA puts the concentrate at about 20% of the feed.
  • Foam fractionation, where air bubbles carry PFAS into a foam. It has been used at full scale on long chains such as PFOS and PFOA, with mixed results on short chains, and is usually considered above 100 ng/L and below about 760 m³/day.

Destruction is narrower. Supercritical water oxidation is field-implemented and best suited to small volumes of liquid concentrates, so far mostly firefighting foam. Electrochemical oxidation and plasma are still being developed for similar streams. New sorbents such as modified clays, zeolites and biochar are in limited use or development.

Ultrashort PFAS such as TFA fall outside the EU sum of 20, though they count under PFAS Total, and they can be harder to remove from water.

The concept, step by step

1. Sample for a full hydrological year

Every water source, both treatment trains, the network, and the wastewater plant inlet, outlet and sludge, through the seasons. Near possible sources, such as sites where firefighting foam was used, and at the wastewater plant, I’d add a TOP assay: a lab test that oxidises many precursors into measurable PFAAs. It shows the potential, not what will happen in the plant. Small column tests on the real water start in parallel.

The lab matters as much as the plan. Checking against a fraction of 0.10 µg/L needs low reporting limits. In the parts of the Georgian accreditation register I checked, I found no lab accredited for PFAS, so the first contract may be with a lab abroad.

2. Treat only where needed

Below roughly half the limit, I’d keep monitoring. That threshold is my engineering judgement; no rule sets it.

Above it, replacing a well or blending comes first, and treatment after. Blending matters more than it looks. Say a source tests at 0.15 µg/L, the target is 0.08, and treated water still carries 0.01. Then treating (0.15 − 0.08) / (0.15 − 0.01) = half the flow is enough. A real design adds margin for seasonal peaks.

3. Carbon on surface water

GAC contactors after the existing filters and before chlorine. Typical design for drinking water is 10 to 20 minutes of empty bed contact time per stage. For every 1 m³/s that is 600 to 1,200 m³ of carbon bed per stage, split into parallel contactors.

A check against a real plant: Wilmington, below, runs 44 MGD (about 1.9 m³/s) at 20 minutes. My estimate gives about 2,300 m³ of bed. Its eight published contactors, 22 by 38 feet with 12.5 feet of carbon, hold about 2,370 m³.

I’d plan two stages in series (lead-lag). It doubles the carbon, but breakthrough is caught between stages and the lead stage can be used to the end. Organic matter in surface water competes with PFAS, so the pilot breakthrough curve sets the final size; contact time is only the starting estimate. Footprint and hydraulic head are the first things I’d check on site, because a new stage after gravity filters often needs pumping.

4. Resin on affected wells

Selective resin suits groundwater with little organic matter, sulfate and nitrate: 1.5 to 3 minutes per stage, or 90 to 180 m³ of resin per stage for each 1 m³/s. A cartridge prefilter goes in front, and disinfection stays after the media.

5. Disposal before technology

Spent carbon goes to reactivation under a dedicated contract. Single-use resin goes to high-temperature incineration or, where permitted, landfill. I couldn’t find public information on such capacity in Georgia, so contracts and logistics have to be settled before the technology is chosen.

I’d also write into the contract what evidence of destruction the receiving facility provides. The EPA’s 2026 draft guidance says uncertainties remain about how completely thermal treatment destroys PFAS, and asks operators to test for products of incomplete combustion at the outlet and for PFAS in all process and waste streams. A recent commentary in Chemical Engineering puts the same idea as a fluorine balance: a PFAS that disappears from one measured stream has not necessarily left the process.

6. Wastewater

Under the 2024 EU urban wastewater directive, authorities must monitor PFAS at the inlets and outlets of plants serving 10,000 population equivalent or more where the effluent reaches a drinking water catchment. There are no limits, and the measurement method is due by January 2027. I’d start with source control at industrial users, which is normally more cost-effective than treating large, dilute flows downstream, and check PFAS in sludge before it is reused.

7. Costs and time

The published capital costs give an order of magnitude only, because scopes differ: about $280 per m³/day of capacity at Wilmington (a 2018 estimate) and about $540 at Anaheim. A real number comes after the pilot, because media life and disposal drive operating cost.

My rough estimate is four to six years from the first samples to operation: about a year of sampling and testing, a year of design and two or more years of construction. Brunswick County, below, shows how far a schedule can slip.

What real utilities have done

Surface water, Wilmington, North Carolina. The Cape Fear Public Utility Authority added eight GAC contactors after the existing filters at its Sweeney plant, rated at 44 MGD (about 166,500 m³/day), with 20 minutes of contact, in October 2022. Its slides give a 2018 capital cost estimate of about $46 million. CFPUA reported that short chains broke through first and ultrashort PFAS were not reliably removed. It now changes carbon on a double trigger, bed volumes or a short-chain indicator, and reactivates it off site.

Surface water, Brunswick County, North Carolina. The county chose low-pressure reverse osmosis after conventional treatment for at least 36 MGD (about 136,000 m³/day), after a 15-month pilot of four membrane products; carbon and resin were among the options rejected. The 2020 construction contract was $122.6 million. Completion has slipped by years and is now expected by the end of 2026. The concentrate is discharged to the Cape Fear River.

Groundwater, Anaheim, California. 14 wells at 9 sites, about 277,000 m³/day: cartridge prefilters, single-use ion exchange in lead-lag, then hypochlorite. Commissioned in 2024 for about $150 million, according to ASCE’s Civil Engineering magazine. In the same basin, the Orange County Water District chose ion exchange for its first treated well after piloting 14 sorbents.

Groundwater, Oakdale, Minnesota. A GAC plant with five lead-lag pairs, designed for about 13,600 m³/day, has run since 2006. PFBA passes the system within about two months of a full carbon change, and the carbon is changed about once a year.

Groundwater, Europe. Uppsala, Sweden, added GAC after sand filters at its Bäcklösa waterworks in 2015. Rastatt, Germany, has run GAC at a waterworks since 2018 and closed another for good; according to BDEW, its PFAS costs reached €24 million by the end of 2024. In Veneto, Italy, the region treats GAC on wells as a temporary measure and is building pipelines to cleaner sources.

On surface water the two cases split, carbon at Wilmington and reverse osmosis at Brunswick. On groundwater, resin or carbon at the wells, and changing the source where possible. Brunswick and Orange County chose their technology only after pilots.

Georgia’s advantage: time

If limits come here later, Georgia won’t have to start from zero. EU utilities have had to meet the drinking water limits since January 2026, and US utilities have been building treatment since the EPA’s 2024 rule. In a few years more technologies will have been run in on real utilities, including the newer ones such as reverse osmosis at Brunswick and destruction of concentrates. A Georgian upgrade could then take the design data and operating experience from those plants and go to the pilot with far fewer unknowns.

What I’d check first

If this brief landed on my desk tomorrow, I’d start with three questions: which sources lie near possible PFAS sources, how much space and head the surface water plant has after its filters, and where spent media could actually go.

For colleagues who added GAC after gravity filters: did you end up pumping, and what decided your carbon change interval?

Calculations (bed volumes, blending, cost per m³/day) and the timeline are my own estimates.
